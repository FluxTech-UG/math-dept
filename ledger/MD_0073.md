## Statement

Let $A = \sum_{e=1}^E A_e$ with $A_e \in \mathbb{C}^{N\times N}$, $Av = \lambda v$,
$w^*A = \lambda w^*$, $w^*v \ne 0$. Put $s_e := w^*A_e v/(w^*v)$. Then $\sum_e s_e = \lambda$, and if
$\lambda$ is algebraically simple then $s_e = \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\lambda(A + \varepsilon A_e)\big|_{\varepsilon=0}$, where $\lambda(A+\varepsilon A_e)$ is the unique eigenvalue of $A + \varepsilon A_e$ near $\lambda$ for small $|\varepsilon|$,
for each $e$.

## Hypotheses

finite dimension; $w^*v \ne 0$ for the sum; algebraic simplicity for the derivative clause.

## In plain words

Write a matrix as the sum of two parts and take an eigenvalue with its right eigenvector and its left eigenvector (the eigenvector of the conjugate transpose), not orthogonal to each other. Sandwich each part between the left and the right eigenvector and divide by the inner product of the two eigenvectors: the two numbers so obtained add up exactly to the eigenvalue. The same holds for any number of parts. When the eigenvalue is simple, each part's share also has a meaning of its own: it is the rate at which the eigenvalue moves when that one part is scaled up slightly while the others stay fixed. The matrix need not be normal. For a non-normal matrix the left eigenvector differs from the right one, and it is the left one that makes each share the true sensitivity: sandwiching with the right eigenvector on both sides still sums to the eigenvalue but loses that meaning.

For the consumer, which splits a linear operator into terms that each stand for one mechanism, this is an exact budget: the real part of an eigenvalue, its growth or decay rate, is the sum of the real parts of the shares, each share is that term's first-order influence on the eigenvalue, and nothing is left over. The sum needs only that the two eigenvectors are not orthogonal; the sensitivity reading needs the eigenvalue to be simple. Both are checked in Lean.
